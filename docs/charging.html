Charging

Public charging

  • RF card: the card supplied with the car is linked to the GridCars app. Load funds in that app, then tap the card. One owner used it without registration on BMW, Mercedes and Rubicon chargers. A single load plus the card was one owner's advice; another suggested loading only on arrival. Refund and expiry terms of other apps were not discussed. The card is a chip: one owner warns not to punch a keyring hole in it.
  • AC limit: the car charges at about 6.6 kW AC (owners say 6.6–7.1 kW). Several owners say three-phase 11/22 kW is not supported, so a 22 kW cable gains nothing.
  • DC: one owner went from 19% to 100% in 40 min; another saw a peak of about 96 kW.
  • Prices per kWh:
    • An Eskom site: about R4.85 (one owner, "I think"). One owner says Eskom seems cheapest.
    • A solar-powered site: R3.49, dropping to R2.49 when its batteries are above 90%.
    • City of Cape Town: R4–R4.50 (one owner).
    • A mall session receipt: 35.11 kWh to 90% SoC for "T 289.12" (tokens; about 8.2 per kWh if 1 token = R1).
  • Charger will not start: two owners at two different DC sites (one with a worn plug) got charging going by holding the plug up, as when pouring fuel, and then locking the car.
  • Apps, route planners and networks: see Providers.

Own cable

  • A unit marked SOCKET needs your own Type 2 AC cable.
  • Combi chargers (AC + DC) usually have a cable attached, "about 80% of the time" per one owner. DC chargers always have a cable. Older AC units often have none, and a solar and battery site on the N4 had six 22 kW AC chargers without cables.
  • BMW, Mercedes and at least one small-town public charger need your own cable.
  • Length: 3 m is considered too short (the car is about 4 m long). Owners suggest 5 m; one bought 8 m.
  • Prices: a 7.4 kW 5 m Type 2 cable with storage bag about R2,600 (delivered in 2 days for one owner); a 22 kW cable about R3,000.

Wall charger (Tellus Power)

  • In the box: a small flat mounting bracket (about matchbox size; two owners missed it at first), 2 RFID cards, an info card and screws. Two owners received the charger from the dealer without a box or manual.
  • To start: tap an RFID card (bottom right of the unit). The app can disable the card requirement so scheduled charging works without tapping (one owner).
  • App setup (one owner's steps): connect the charger to wifi, then Me > Bluetooth > select the charger > Basic. Rated and maximum current can be adjusted; press "write" after each change, then reboot the charger.
  • Lights and switch: red = fault, blue = standby, green = charging. The red knob on the side works as a cut-off switch (a fault showed until it was turned).
  • Faults reported: one new installation shows a green port light on the car but a steady blue light on the box, and the app "starts" charging with no effect (unresolved). One owner gets an error when updating the firmware.
  • App feedback: see Providers.

Current and wiring

Owner discussion, not electrical advice. Have a qualified electrician inspect the installation and issue a compliance certificate.

  • Owners' current settings and cable sizes differ: see Conflicting info.
  • One owner's distribution board feels warm while charging; an electrician was booked to check.
  • Other factors raised: cable run length, the size of the feed to any sub-board, and loose connections, which high current exposes.
  • One owner's installer used only the top 2 mounting screws; check the unit is fully fixed.

Portable charger (3-pin)

  • Owners report 1,000 W to 2.5–3.2 kW, depending on the circuit breaker and socket. The Q has no setting for the charging current.
  • One owner's portable charger melted a 16 A plug; poor contact at the plug or under-rated wiring was suggested. Check the socket's rating and earthing.

Plug stuck in the car

  • Owners fixed it by unlocking the car 2–3 times, or by ending charging in the menu and unlocking. At a charger that needs a card, tap the card to release.
  • A member's summary of the manual:
    • Charging finished, car unlocked: plug releases by itself.
    • Charging finished, car locked: unlock the car with the key, then pull the plug.
    • Charging not finished, car locked: press unlock on the key twice in a row.
    • Charging not finished, car unlocked: stop charging on the screen (energy management) or at the charger.
    • Never pull the plug by force. At a DC charger, stop the session first.
  • Emergency release (lock fault): confirm nothing is charging, open the back door, lift the left luggage compartment cover flap and pull the electronic lock cable, then unplug. Do not use it while charging or if orange high-voltage cable is exposed, and not often. If still stuck, call Chery or roadside assistance.

V2L

  • Output: about 3.3 kW (about 15 A) through a third-party adapter (not supplied; see Providers). A fridge should run easily. Owners have run a kettle, woodworking tools, camp lights, an air pump, a laptop and a 220 V fridge.
  • Buy the right adapter: it must fit the car's inlet. One owner's adapter from an online retailer plugged into a charger, not the car (wrong pinout) and was returned. An adapter from a charging retailer, rated 3.6 kW, works for several owners (about R1,500).
  • Heat and cabling: full output at 15 A heats the connection points. Owners advise quality connectors (Crabtree type) and FTe (flat twin and earth) or Surfix cable, not Cabtyre, which an owner says is not allowed as a fixed installation.
  • Cut-off: one owner says V2L stops at 30% battery.
  • Into a home inverter: one installer limited output to 2 kW and says it is "not recommended for daily use", only as emergency input. Whether V2L cycling affects the battery warranty is unknown.
  • One owner estimates a 120–200 W ice maker would use about 4% battery over 8 hours.

Battery

  • Owners have seen 3–4% as the lowest. One reports driving 6+ km slowly at 0%.
